### Post Template 1: Pricing Strategy
**Title:** "What Sells Fast vs. What Sits: Real Data from 40 Sales"

**Copy:**
> After coordinating 40 estate and garage sales in the last year, I've noticed the same pricing patterns. Sharing because it might help your next event.
>
> Items that move fast:
> - Functional furniture (bed frames, dressers, tables) priced 25-30% below retail
> - Vintage kitchen items under $20
> - Complete collectible sets (full china patterns, book series)
> - Electronics that are <3 years old (visible condition helps)
>
> Items that sit or tank in price:
> - Worn upholstered furniture (couches, chairs with stains)
> - Broken glass items or chipped vintage
> - Individual orphaned dishes or stemware
> - CDs, DVDs, VHS (bulk discount to <$1 or donate)
>
> The biggest lever: photography and description quality. A $60 lamp with a blurry photo and vague description? Sits. The same lamp with clear photos and "1970s brass, teal shade, works perfectly"? Gone in 2 days.
>
> Photography doesn't have to be professional — just clear, well-lit, true color.

**Engagement prompt:** "What categories surprise you? What sits at your sales that I haven't mentioned?"

---

### Post Template 2: Photography & Online Presence
**Title:** "The 3-Photo Minimum That Gets Serious Buyers"

**Copy:**
> Not everyone is hiring a photographer for their sale. And that's fine. But if you're listing online, even a garage sale, three photos of each item matter.
>
> 1. Full-view shot (shows the whole item, true color, scale). Phone camera, natural light, plain background.
> 2. Detail shot (highlight condition, maker's mark, serial number if it matters). Close-up shows care.
> 3. Context shot (item in a room or styled setup if relevant). Helps buyers envision placement.
>
> The sellers I see moving the most inventory online aren't the ones with studio photography. They're the ones who show the real condition honestly (no filtered/edited photos) and describe specifically what makes something worth the price.
>
> Bad example: "Vintage desk. $100." Good example: "Mid-century wood desk, 48x24, drawers all work, minor surface marks (shown in 3rd photo), $100."
>
> Time investment: 15 minutes for 20 items if you batch it. Worth it.

**Engagement prompt:** "Do you use outdoor light or indoor for your photos? Natural light is simpler than I thought until someone showed me how."

---

### Post Template 3: Handling Difficult Moments (Family Conflict)
**Title:** "When Family Disagrees on Pricing: What We've Learned"

**Copy:**
> This one comes up constantly in estate sales. Adult kids think "Grandma's antique should be $500." The person managing the sale (often a surviving spouse) needs it gone and priced at $40.
>
> A few frameworks that have helped:
>
> **1. Get it in writing early.** Before the sale opens, have ONE conversation about pricing philosophy. "We're pricing to move items, not to maximize heirloom value." Document that decision.
>
> **2. Use market comps.** Show what similar items actually sold for on eBay or Etsy. "These chairs: identical set sold last month for $150, not $500." Facts > feelings.
>
> **3. Separate emotional items from sale items.** If Uncle Bob thinks his grandfather's watch should be kept or go to the museum, that's different from pricing a watch for sale. Agree on that upfront.
>
> **4. Have a markdown plan.** "Week 1: full price. Week 2: 20% off. Week 3: 40% off. Final day: donation and clear-out." Removes the argument. It's the schedule, not the person deciding.
>
> **5. Let the market decide on holdouts.** Stuff nobody buys tells you something.
>
> These conflicts are the hardest part of the job. Pricing is easier once you agree on goals.

**Engagement prompt:** "How do you handle it? Have you had to make tough calls between what family wanted and what made sense?"

### Post Template 4: Inventory at Scale
**Title:** "Managing 500+ Items Without Losing Your Mind"

**Copy:**
> Large sales (multi-day auctions, flea markets, estate sale companies with 4-5 concurrent sales) require a different system than a backyard garage sale. Here's what separates organized from chaotic:
>
> **The bottleneck:** Not the items themselves. It's knowing what you have, where it is, and what it's worth.
>
> **What works:**
> - One centralized tracking method (not spreadsheets on 3 devices)
> - Photo of each item (yes, even the $3 items)
> - Consistent categories (don't have "Kitchen" and "Kitchenware" at the same time)
> - Clear condition notes ("works," "works with scuff," "broken handle," "missing parts")
> - Pricing rule of thumb so you're not repricing every item individually
>
> **What doesn't work:**
> - Trying to remember details
> - Inconsistent category naming
> - Blurry photos
> - Pricing items in real-time during setup
>
> Time investment: Batch photography and tagging the day before or day-of setup. Category and pricing as you go.
>
> If you're running 500+ item sales, this system becomes your second full-time job during setup. Anything that cuts that time in half is worth exploring.

**Engagement prompt:** "How do you manage large inventories? Any systems that have worked better than others?"

### Post Template 5: What Sells vs. What Doesn't (Collections)
**Title:** "Collectibles That Move vs. Collectibles That Sit"

**Copy:**
> As someone coordinating sales, I see the difference between collections that attract buyers and collections that clog the sale.
>
> **Collections that move:**
> - Complete sets (full china patterns, book series, matching dishes)
> - Named/branded collectibles (Hummel figurines, Precious Moments, Dept. 56)
> - Functional + collectible (vintage kitchen tools that people actually use)
> - Trending categories (mid-century furniture, vintage camper/RV decor, 1980s-90s nostalgia)
>
> **Collections that sit:**
> - Partial sets (3 orphan pieces from a china pattern)
> - Hyper-specific collections (20 items from a single regional county fair, 10 items from a specific TV show)
> - Sentimental-only items (the whole collection is just "my grandma collected this")
> - Items priced at collector value when you need to move volume
>
> The honest truth: Not all collections are worth the table space. If it doesn't complete a buyer's existing collection or match their decor style, it's just inventory taking up space.
>
> Strategy: Price complete sets and trending items normally. Everything else? Bulk discount or donation.

**Engagement prompt:** "What collections have surprised you by either flying off the shelf or sitting forever?"

### Post Template 6: Three Days Before Opening (Logistics Checklist)
**Title:** "The Unwritten Checklist: 72 Hours Before You Open"

**Copy:**
> The night before a sale opens (online or in-person), here's what separates smooth from chaotic:
>
> **Inventory & listing prep:**
> - [ ] All items photographed and listed (or at least 80%+)
> - [ ] Pricing is done (not a moving target)
> - [ ] Major categories assigned
> - [ ] Obvious mistakes fixed (typos, duplicate listings, wrong photos)
>
> **Logistics:**
> - [ ] Sale location is confirmed (and hours are published)
> - [ ] Items are arranged or at least organized by zone
> - [ ] Payment method is set up and tested
> - [ ] You have cash for change
> - [ ] Signage is ready (if applicable)
>
> **Communication:**
> - [ ] Announce the sale (email list, social, word-of-mouth)
> - [ ] Answer any pre-sale questions
> - [ ] Set expectations (hours, parking, payment types, whether you allow holds)
>
> **Mental prep:**
> - [ ] You've slept (seriously)
> - [ ] You're not trying to price items 2 hours before opening
> - [ ] You have a backup plan if something breaks (payment processor, power, etc.)
>
> Most chaos comes from leaving setup for the last minute. If inventory is done 48 hours before opening, you can actually breathe.

**Engagement prompt:** "What's one thing you always forget that bites you later? Or one thing you do that makes it smooth?"

## 7. Brand Voice Guardrails for This Playbook

- **Peer, not pitchy:** You're sharing what works because you've lived it, not because you're selling.
- **No "AI" language:** Use "auto-tags," "smart pricing," "suggested" — never "AI."
- **Specific examples:** Always cite real numbers (27 minutes, 150 items, $2,000 sale) — never generic benefits.
- **Honest about limitations:** Acknowledge when it's not the right fit (small one-time garage sale, free Craigslist preference, etc.).
- **All sale types equally:** Don't treat estate sales as default. Estate, garage, flea, auction — all legitimate.
- **Humor and warmth:** Be human. Estate sale coordinators have dark humor about the work. Don't pretend it's not emotional or hard.
